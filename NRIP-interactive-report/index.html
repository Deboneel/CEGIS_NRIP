One kind of clay decides how 175,933 hectares get irrigated.

Beneath the North Rajshahi Irrigation Project lies an old, tight, slow-draining clay — and everything about how this land is farmed traces back to it. This is the story of how a GIS map and a crop-water model found one narrow, precise fix hiding inside a much bigger question.

THE QUESTION

Why does the same irrigation plan work in one field and fail in the next?

The North Rajshahi Irrigation Project traces back to a 1998 feasibility study by BWDB and JICA. This report picks that question back up with today's GIS layers and a modern crop-water model, AquaCrop — and lands on an answer narrow enough to actually act on.

What was mapped

Seven biophysical layers, cropping patterns, and irrigation infrastructure across the High and Level Barind Tract around Rajshahi, Chapainawabganj, and Naogaon.

Why it matters

Boro rice takes the largest irrigated share and the highest cost of any crop here — because unlike the monsoon crops, none of its water comes free from the sky.

How it was answered

The same rice, the same weather, thirty-five growing seasons — with only the soil and the irrigation trigger changing between runs.

Soil texture — not the irrigation schedule — decides whether water management moves the needle here.
HOW WE GOT HERE

From a 1998 feasibility study to a season of fieldwork

CEGIS's history with this project runs deep. Here's the short version of the path — and the toolkit — that this report is built on.

1998

The original plan

BWDB and JICA split the project area into the low, flat Paba plain and the raised Barind Tract, since the two behave completely differently underground. The Barind Tract needed water pumped uphill from the Ganges — expensive, and still shaping the area's water economy today.

Orientation

Learning the ground rules

CEGIS's DRAS drought-assessment tool and a session with BADC's engineering team set the baseline: what irrigation infrastructure exists here, and where it quietly runs into friction — like solar pumps needing power lines rural grids don't have.

Mapping

Six layers, one geology

Agro-ecological zone, soil texture, drainage, moisture, water recession, land type — seven thematic layers built in ArcGIS Pro, each telling the same story about this land from a different angle.

Simulation

AquaCrop enters the picture

FAO's crop-water model ran the same rice variety across every soil type and irrigation schedule the Barind Tract actually uses — turning a map of where the clay sits into a number for what the clay costs.

THE LAND

175,933 hectares, nine upazilas, one dominant geology

Three districts, mostly one landscape: the High Barind Tract — elevated old terrace land with hard, reddish clay subsoil that drains slowly. That single trait explains almost everything that follows.

The one fact behind everything else

The High Barind Tract's old, tight clay subsoil holds water poorly, needs irrigation even in normal-rainfall years, and pushed the whole area toward groundwater as its default water source.

Seven layers, read side by side

Tap through the tabs — each one pairs the spatial map with its own quick breakdown.

Agro-ecological zone

Two zones dominate: the High Barind Tract (64.7%) and the Level Barind Tract (22.7%). Together they cover 87% of NRIP — small floodplain fringes sit only at the margins.

Soil texture

Clay loam leads at 51.0%, with loam close behind at 33.4%. Pure clay is a small slice — just 7.5% — but it behaves so differently under irrigation that it needs its own rule.

Drainage characteristics

84.2% is imperfectly drained — the fingerprint of old terrace clay sitting over hard subsoil, not an active floodplain.

Soil moisture

69.8% sits in the low-moisture class — the subsoil won't hold water once the surface dries, despite the clay content.

Surface water recession

80.3% recedes very early — surface water pulls back well before the dry season starts, pushing Boro onto pumped groundwater almost by default.

Land type

81.0% highland — the elevated terrace signature of the Barind Tract. Almost nothing here floods seasonally the way lowland regions do.

Landform

Terrace dominates at 62.8%. Godagari and Niamatpur — the biggest upazilas, most fully inside the Barind Tract — lead every layer simply by size and position.

Total mapped area by upazila

Godagari and Niamatpur lead simply by being the biggest, sitting most fully within the High Barind Tract.
Reading the seven layers together

None of these seven statistics contradicts another — they all describe the same old, elevated, slow-draining geology from a different angle. The consequence is narrow but stable: the land suits rice, and little else, because clay retains water well enough for paddy but compacts and cracks under any crop that needs aeration.

Where Boro rice actually grows

115,429 hectares, 484,802 tons — concentrated in Niamatpur, Godagari, and Tanore, the upazilas with the most complete groundwater infrastructure.

Why Boro costs so much more

Boro costs 2.5–3× more to irrigate than most other crops. Unlike Aus or Aman, all of its water demand falls inside the dry season — rainfall gives nothing back. Every millimetre is pumped, which is exactly why the soil differences ahead translate directly into cost, not just yield.

Boro's water comes almost entirely from pumping. Climate stress won't show up as crop failure here — it'll show up as a bigger pumping bill.

The tension underneath the coverage numbers

Niamatpur and Godagari draw the most groundwater and lead Boro production — the same pattern seen nationally in Rajshahi and Rangpur, where high coverage and deepening water tables move together. Pumping this hard in a landscape this slow to recharge is an imbalance that compounds over years, even if no single season shows it.

THE SOIL

Same rice. Same weather. Only the soil changed — and everything else followed.

An AquaCrop simulation ran the same 110-day rice variety across 35 growing seasons, on all five standard soil types, under three irrigation rhythms. Here's what the soil alone was responsible for.

Why clay is the outlier: water moves through it 4 to 34 times slower than the other four soils, and its safety margin before waterlogging sets in is razor-thin. The rice variety's own tolerance threshold sits inside that margin — so clay crosses into stress whenever it's kept too wet.

What actually gets applied

Under a frequent rhythm, clay needs the least water of the five — its slow drainage holds moisture longer between waterings. Switch to infrequent, and the ranking flips entirely: sandy loam now needs the most, because it simply can't hold a reserve the way clay can.

What actually drives yield

Clay's yield penalty under frequent watering is a stress effect, not a shortage effect — it gets less water and is still worse off, because staying wet too long is the actual problem. Water it less often, and that penalty mostly disappears.

Why yield rises without more water

Three things line up: rising CO₂ lifts how efficiently this rice converts water into grain; that water-productivity gain grows even faster than yield itself; and a longer, warmer growing window lets more biomass build up before harvest — all without asking for meaningfully more water.

Read this carefully before generalizing

This result is Boro rice on clay loam only. Pure clay behaves differently under fixed irrigation schedules, so these gains can't be assumed to carry over there without testing it directly. The rest of the crop-and-soil matrix is still open.

THE VERDICT

It's a soil-texture story, not a scheduling story — except in one place

Pull every thread together and this lands on one narrow, testable fix rather than a sweeping call to overhaul irrigation everywhere.

The 84% case — nothing to fix

Clay loam, loam, silty loam, and sandy loam — roughly 84% of the cultivable land — hold Boro yield steady no matter how often the field is watered. Pushing a schedule change everywhere would be solving a problem that mostly doesn't exist here.

The 7.5% case — a precise fix needed

Pure clay breaks the pattern under frequent watering, then falls back in line once watering becomes infrequent. The mechanism is consistent throughout: clay simply holds water too long when refilled too often.

The recommendation, in one sentence

Switch to Alternate Wetting and Drying only in the upazilas where clay dominates — Tanore, Godagari, Chapainawabganj Sadar, and Bagmara — rather than everywhere. That way, the fix actually lands where the evidence says it's needed.

What else is on the table

A map on its own can show where clay sits. A simulation on its own can show a soil is stressed. Neither tells you what to actually recommend until you connect them.
What's still open

The climate results cover only Boro rice on clay loam. Five other crops and four remaining soil-and-climate combinations are still unmodelled — this is a first, place-specific piece of a bigger picture, not a finished one.

THE TAKEAWAY

Three tools, used separately, don't answer the question. Used together, they do.

A map, a crop model, and a season of fieldwork — none of them alone gets to a recommendation this precise. Together, they turned a 175,933-hectare question into a four-upazila answer.
